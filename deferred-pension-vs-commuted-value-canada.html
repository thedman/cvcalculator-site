Deferred pension vs. commuted value — a real comparison with numbers

Two common options when leaving a defined-benefit pension are retaining a pension for later payment and making an eligible commuted-value transfer. A transfer to another pension plan or other routes may also be available. This guide compares cash flows using a simplified illustration, not a personal recommendation.

By the CVCalculator Team · Updated November 15, 2026 · 7 min read

What you are actually comparing

A deferred pension is the benefit you have earned and left in the plan for later payment. Starting dates, reductions, inflation adjustments and survivor benefits follow plan rules. A commuted-value transfer replaces the corresponding pension entitlement with funds managed under the receiving arrangement's rules.

You are comparing a guaranteed income stream against the potential returns from self-managed investing. Neither is automatically better. The right answer depends on your specific numbers and circumstances.

A simplified comparison — $3,000/month pension, age 45

Illustration, not a pension quote

Assume a member aged 45 has a pension of $36,000 a year starting at 60, without inflation increases or survivor benefits. For the investment illustration, assume $396,000 is already available in a locked-in account. That starting amount is an assumption, not a calculated CV or section 8517 transfer limit.

Pension cash flows: 20 full years of $36,000 payments total $720,000 before tax; 25 years total $900,000; 30 years total $1,080,000. These are cumulative payments, not present values.

Investment cash flows: no additions or withdrawals for 15 years, followed by $36,000 withdrawn at each year-end. Assume the same constant nominal return before and after age 60, no fees, no inflation increases and no tax deductions within this arithmetic model. Any separate taxable excess and its investment proceeds are excluded. Both pension payments and locked-in withdrawals can be taxable; this is not an after-tax income comparison.

At 4%, $396,000 × 1.0415 = approximately $713,174 at age 60. After each year's growth, subtract $36,000. Under these assumptions, the balance first becomes insufficient for the full withdrawal after 41 years, around age 101.

At 6%, $396,000 × 1.0615 = approximately $949,037. Initial annual growth exceeds $36,000, so the account does not deplete under this constant-return model. This is not a promise that real investments will earn 6% or that funds will last indefinitely.

Actual locked-in withdrawal limits, tax, fees, variable returns and pension features can materially change the comparison. This arithmetic illustration is not a permitted LIF withdrawal schedule or an actuarial valuation.

Depletion and break-even are different questions

Portfolio depletion asks when withdrawals exhaust invested funds. A cumulative-payment crossover asks when two defined totals become equal. Neither is a complete measure of pension value. Specify the comparison, taxes and cash-flow timing before interpreting any “break-even age.”

Use several scenarios and consider lifetime income, survivor protections and inflation alongside the numbers. Constant returns are a teaching assumption, not an investment forecast.

Other factors to consider

Favours taking the CV

Investment discipline

If you have a consistent long-term investment track record and will not panic-sell in downturns, the CV has a realistic chance of outperforming.

Favours keeping the pension

Longevity

A lifetime pension continues under its terms even if you live longer than expected. That protection cannot be summarized by a single investment crossover age.

Favours taking the CV

Estate value

Death benefits and rights of a spouse or beneficiary depend on pension law and account terms. Compare those rules with the plan's survivor and guarantee benefits rather than assuming one route always provides a larger estate.

Favours keeping the pension

Indexation

Inflation protection can be valuable, but its guaranteed or conditional level depends on the plan and service period. Check the protection you would retain or surrender.

Use official information and independent analysis

Your administrator confirms your benefits, eligibility and official amounts. Independent advice and educational tools can help you examine assumptions and prepare questions; neither replaces the official package.

One comparison the numbers cannot make

Investment risk is real. A deferred pension transfers all investment and longevity risk to the plan. A commuted value transfers all of that risk to you. If you invest poorly, or if markets deliver a lost decade at the wrong time, the deferred pension that felt like the lesser option at age 45 may look far better at age 70.

The worked example above assumes consistent returns — real investing does not work that way. This is the honest limit of any calculator comparison.

Frequently asked questions

Is a deferred pension better than taking the commuted value in Canada?

Neither is universally better. Compare eligibility, lifetime and survivor income, tax, inflation protection, investment risk and your circumstances. A scenario result does not replace professional advice.

What is a deferred pension in Canada?

It is a pension benefit earned and left in the plan for payment later. Starting dates, adjustments and reductions depend on the plan.

Can you take a commuted value instead of a deferred pension in Canada?

Only where the applicable plan and pension rules permit it. There is no universal two-year vesting period or age-55 cutoff. Ask your administrator to confirm eligibility, dates and available routes.

How is commuted value different from a deferred pension?

A commuted value is a lump sum present value of your future pension payments. A deferred pension is the monthly income itself, paid for life starting at retirement. The commuted value is typically split between a tax-sheltered LIRA transfer and a taxable cash payment. The deferred pension is fully taxable as income when received in retirement.
